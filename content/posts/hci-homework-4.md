## Lecture 10: The Evolution of Smartphone Shapes and Their Future Development

### 1. Introduction

The physical shape of mobile phones has changed significantly over the past few decades. From bulky devices with physical keyboards to modern smartphones with large touchscreens, these transformations have been driven by technological development, changing user needs, and improvements in human-computer interaction (HCI).

In my opinion, the evolution of smartphone shapes is not simply about making devices thinner or more attractive. It reflects a continuous attempt to balance three important factors: portability, display area, and interaction efficiency. As smartphones become increasingly important for communication, entertainment, education, and professional activities, their physical designs must adapt to more diverse usage scenarios.

I believe that the next important development could be smartphones with dynamically adjustable shapes, particularly rollable smartphones that can change their screen size according to the user&#39;s current needs.

### 2. The Historical Evolution of Smartphone Shapes

Early mobile phones were primarily designed for voice communication. They usually had relatively small screens, physical number pads, and thick bodies containing batteries and electronic components. Since their main functions were making calls and sending short messages, a large display was not necessary.

Later, mobile phones adopted different physical designs, including flip phones, sliding phones, and devices with full QWERTY keyboards. These variations attempted to improve portability, protect the screen, or make text input more convenient. For example, sliding keyboards provided more space for typing without permanently increasing the device&#39;s overall size.

A major transformation occurred with the widespread adoption of capacitive touchscreens and mobile operating systems. Physical keyboards were gradually replaced by software keyboards, allowing the same display to support different input methods depending on the application.

This change was particularly important from an HCI perspective. Instead of designing separate physical buttons for different functions, developers could create flexible graphical user interfaces. A smartphone could function as a communication device, camera, navigation system, media player, or productivity tool without requiring a different physical control layout for each activity.

As mobile internet services, video streaming, social media, and mobile applications became more popular, manufacturers increasingly adopted larger displays and thinner bezels. Modern smartphones therefore developed toward a relatively standardized rectangular shape, with a large touchscreen occupying most of the front surface.

However, this design introduced a fundamental limitation: the ideal screen size for consuming information is not necessarily the ideal device size for carrying and holding it.

### 3. Why Smartphone Shapes Continue to Change

I think smartphone shapes evolve mainly because of three interconnected factors.

First, technological progress creates new design possibilities. Improvements in display manufacturing, battery technology, electronic integration, and materials allow manufacturers to create thinner devices with larger screens. More recently, flexible OLED technology has made foldable and rollable display concepts technically possible.

Second, users increasingly expect smartphones to support different activities. A compact phone is convenient for answering messages while walking, but a larger screen is more comfortable for reading academic papers, editing documents, watching videos, or using multiple applications simultaneously.

Third, interaction methods influence physical design. The transition from physical keyboards to touchscreens demonstrated how changes in input technology could fundamentally transform device shapes. Future developments in voice interaction, gesture recognition, and artificial intelligence may further reduce the dependence on fixed physical controls.

These factors suggest that smartphone design will continue moving toward devices that can accommodate different activities without requiring users to carry several separate products.

### 4. My Prediction: A Rollable Smartphone with an Adaptive Screen

I believe that one possible next stage of smartphone evolution is a rollable smartphone whose display can expand or retract according to the user&#39;s current task.

Unlike conventional smartphones, which have a fixed display area, this device would combine the portability of a compact phone with some of the advantages of a small tablet.

#### 4.1 Physical Shape and Structure

In its compact state, the device could resemble a conventional smartphone with an approximately 6-inch display. It would have a rectangular body, rounded edges, and a thickness suitable for everyday carrying.

However, part of its flexible OLED display would be stored inside the device, wrapped around a carefully designed rolling mechanism.

When additional screen space is needed, a movable section of the phone&#39;s body would extend sideways, allowing the flexible display to emerge gradually.

For example, the screen could expand from approximately 6 inches to 8 inches diagonally, depending on the display geometry and the mechanical design.

Unlike a traditional foldable phone, which generally switches between a limited number of configurations, a rollable smartphone could potentially support several intermediate screen sizes.

#### 4.2 A Concrete Usage Scenario

Imagine that I am travelling to university in the morning.

While walking to the station, I use the phone in its compact form to check messages, view transportation information, and listen to music. The smaller device is easy to hold with one hand and fits conveniently into my pocket.

After sitting down on the train, I decide to read an academic paper. Instead of repeatedly zooming into a small PDF page, I activate the screen expansion function.

A miniature motor gradually extends the flexible display, increasing the available screen area. The document automatically adjusts to the larger screen, making text, diagrams, and equations easier to read.

Later, when I need to compare information from two applications, I expand the screen further and activate a split-screen interface. One side displays the academic paper, while the other shows my notes.

When I arrive at university, I close the document and retract the screen. The device returns to its compact form without requiring me to carry a separate tablet.

This scenario illustrates how a single device could physically adapt to different activities throughout the day.

#### 4.3 Technical Principles

Several technologies would be necessary to make this concept practical.

The most important component is a flexible OLED display. Unlike conventional rigid displays, flexible OLED panels can be manufactured on bendable substrates, allowing them to curve around a rolling mechanism within specified mechanical limits.

A precision mechanical system, potentially driven by miniature electric motors, would control the extension and retraction of the screen. Guide rails and supporting structures would help maintain the flatness and stability of the expanded display.

Position sensors could measure the extension of the screen and communicate its current dimensions to the operating system.

The software would then use a responsive layout system to reorganize interface elements according to the available display area. For example, a messaging application might show a single conversation in compact mode but display the conversation list and message content side by side in expanded mode.

Touch input would also need to remain accurate throughout the transformation, requiring coordination between the display, touch-sensing system, and operating system.

The result would be a device in which hardware transformation and software adaptation operate together.

### 5. HCI Implications and Remaining Challenges

From an HCI perspective, the most important advantage of this concept is its ability to adapt the physical interface to the user&#39;s task.

Traditional smartphones require users to perform different activities within a fixed screen size. A rollable smartphone could instead allow the user to choose a more appropriate display area for each situation.

This could improve readability, multitasking, and flexibility while preserving portability.

However, an adaptive physical design would also introduce new interaction challenges. Users would need to understand when and how the device should expand. The transformation should be predictable, easy to control, and safe. Accidental activation could be inconvenient, particularly when the device is being carried or held tightly.

Mechanical durability would also be a major concern. Repeated rolling could damage the flexible display, while dust, impacts, and mechanical wear could affect the expansion mechanism.

In addition, manufacturers would need to consider battery consumption, device thickness, production costs, and the long-term reliability of moving components.

Therefore, although the underlying technologies already exist in experimental or commercial forms, making such devices affordable, durable, and widely accessible remains challenging.

### 6. Conclusion

The evolution of smartphone shapes demonstrates how technological progress and human needs continuously influence one another. Mobile phones have developed from devices with physical buttons and small displays into multifunctional touchscreen computers, and more recently into foldable designs.

I believe that the next stage could involve more adaptable physical interfaces, such as rollable smartphones that change their screen size according to different activities.

The central idea is not simply to create a larger screen, but to allow the device to adjust its physical form to the user&#39;s needs.

From an HCI perspective, this represents a possible transition from users adapting to fixed devices toward devices adapting to users and their changing contexts.

Although rollable smartphones may not become the dominant design immediately, they illustrate a promising direction for future human-computer interaction.

## Lecture 11: The Computer for the 21st Century — Progress Toward Ubiquitous Computing

### 1. Introduction

In his 1991 paper The Computer for the 21st Century, Mark Weiser proposed a vision of ubiquitous computing in which computers would become deeply integrated into everyday environments. Rather than remaining isolated machines that require people&#39;s continuous attention, computing systems would be distributed throughout the physical world and naturally support human activities.

More than three decades later, this vision has become increasingly relevant. Smartphones, wearable devices, smart home systems, wireless networks, and cloud computing have made digital technology accessible almost everywhere. However, the widespread availability of computing devices does not necessarily mean that ubiquitous computing has been fully achieved.

In my opinion, we are already living in an early stage of ubiquitous computing, but we have not yet realized Weiser&#39;s complete vision. Although the technological infrastructure has developed significantly, modern computing still depends heavily on explicit user interactions, fragmented devices, and interfaces that compete for human attention.

The next stage of development should therefore focus not simply on increasing the number of computers, but on making computing more integrated, context-aware, interoperable, and unobtrusive.

### 2. Weiser&#39;s Vision of Ubiquitous Computing

Weiser&#39;s central argument was that the most successful technologies eventually become so deeply integrated into everyday life that people no longer consciously notice them.

He illustrated this idea through the example of electric motors. In the past, factories often relied on large centralized engines to operate multiple machines. As electric motors became smaller and more affordable, they were incorporated into individual tools and appliances. Today, people use these motors without consciously thinking about the technology behind them.

Weiser believed that computing could undergo a similar transformation.

Instead of interacting with one dominant personal computer, people would live in environments containing many interconnected computing devices. These devices would support everyday activities without constantly demanding attention.

To illustrate this concept, Weiser described three categories of computing devices:

- Tabs: Small, inch-scale devices resembling electronic notes or identification badges.

- Pads: Portable, paper-sized computing surfaces that could be used as naturally as ordinary sheets of paper.

- Boards: Large interactive displays integrated into walls or shared environments.

Importantly, these devices were not intended to operate independently. Their value would emerge from their ability to communicate and cooperate within a shared physical environment.

Weiser also emphasized the importance of location and scale. Computing devices should understand their positions and surroundings so that they can respond appropriately to different situations.

He referred to the integration of digital information into the physical world as embodied virtuality, distinguishing it from virtual reality systems that attempt to replace the surrounding environment with a simulated one.

Therefore, the fundamental objective of ubiquitous computing is not simply to make computers smaller or more numerous. It is to create environments in which computing becomes a natural and almost invisible part of human activity.

### 3. Technological Progress Toward Ubiquitous Computing

Several important technological developments have brought modern society closer to Weiser&#39;s vision.

#### 3.1 Mobile and Wearable Computing

Smartphones, tablets, and smartwatches demonstrate how computing has moved beyond traditional desktop environments.

These devices allow people to access information, communicate, navigate, and monitor activities across different locations.

Although they are not identical to Weiser&#39;s tabs and pads, they reflect his prediction that computing devices would become smaller, portable, and available in different physical forms.

Wearable devices represent an additional step toward continuous computing because they can collect information and provide assistance without requiring users to carry or operate a conventional computer.

#### 3.2 Internet of Things and Embedded Computing

The Internet of Things (IoT) has extended computing capabilities into ordinary physical objects.

Smart thermostats, connected lighting systems, environmental sensors, and intelligent appliances can monitor conditions and perform automated actions.

For example, a smart heating system can adjust indoor temperatures using sensor measurements and predefined user preferences.

This resembles Weiser&#39;s expectation that computing would become embedded in everyday objects rather than remaining confined to recognizable computers.

#### 3.3 Wireless Networks and Cloud Computing

Modern wireless communication technologies allow large numbers of devices to exchange information without relying on permanent physical connections.

Cloud computing further enables applications and data to be accessed across different devices.

Together, these technologies provide much of the infrastructure required for distributed computing environments.

However, network connectivity alone does not guarantee seamless cooperation. Devices may be connected to the internet while still operating within separate software ecosystems.

#### 3.4 Sensors and Context-Aware Systems

Modern devices increasingly incorporate cameras, microphones, accelerometers, positioning systems, and environmental sensors.

These components allow computing systems to obtain information about their surroundings and adapt their behavior.

For example, a smartphone can automatically change its screen orientation, while a navigation application can provide location-dependent information.

Such capabilities represent important progress toward the context-aware computing environments envisioned by Weiser.

### 4. Limitations of Contemporary Computing Environments

Despite these developments, I believe that several important differences remain between contemporary computing and Weiser&#39;s original vision.

#### 4.1 Fragmented Devices and Limited Interoperability

Although people may own multiple connected devices, these systems do not always cooperate smoothly.

Different manufacturers use different software platforms, communication protocols, and data formats. As a result, users often need to configure connections, transfer files, or manage applications manually.

In Weiser&#39;s vision, computing devices would function as coordinated components of a shared environment rather than as separate technological products.

#### 4.2 Continuous Demands on Human Attention

Modern smartphones and computers frequently require direct interaction through screens, applications, and notifications.

Users must repeatedly unlock devices, navigate menus, and switch between applications to complete relatively simple tasks.

Furthermore, digital services often compete for attention through notifications and personalized content.

This creates a contradiction: computing technology has become increasingly widespread, but it has not necessarily become less intrusive.

Weiser expected ubiquitous computing to help reduce information overload by integrating information into the surrounding environment. In contrast, contemporary digital systems can sometimes intensify information overload.

#### 4.3 Privacy, Security, and User Autonomy

A truly context-aware environment would require access to information about people&#39;s locations, activities, preferences, and interactions.

While this information could improve convenience, it would also create significant privacy and security risks.

For example, a system that automatically recognizes individuals and monitors their activities could become intrusive if users cannot understand or control how their data is collected and processed.

Therefore, ubiquitous computing should not be confused with unrestricted environmental surveillance.

Users must retain meaningful control over sensing, data sharing, and automated decisions.

### 5. Future Developments and Enabling Technologies

To move closer to Weiser&#39;s vision, several technological and social developments are necessary.

First, stronger interoperability is essential. Devices from different manufacturers should be able to communicate through compatible standards and exchange information without complicated manual configuration.

Second, computing systems need more advanced context awareness. Future systems should combine information from multiple sensors to understand activities, objects, and environmental conditions more accurately.

Artificial intelligence could contribute to this process by interpreting contextual information and selecting appropriate responses. However, automated behavior should remain predictable and controllable.

Third, computing interfaces should become less dependent on fixed screens and explicit commands. Natural interaction methods, including speech, gestures, tangible interfaces, and spatial displays, could allow people to interact with digital information while remaining engaged with their physical surroundings.

Fourth, privacy-preserving computing must become a fundamental design principle. Local data processing, selective information sharing, secure authentication, and transparent permission systems could help reduce the risks associated with continuous environmental sensing.

Finally, future systems should be designed around human attention and autonomy. The objective should not be to automate every possible activity, but to provide useful assistance when appropriate while allowing people to remain in control.

### 6. A Future Scenario: Ambient Intelligence and Invisible Interaction

To illustrate how ubiquitous computing might develop further, I imagine a research workspace in 2045 where computing is integrated throughout the physical environment.

Instead of relying on a conventional desktop computer, the workspace contains distributed processors, embedded sensors, interactive surfaces, and spatial visualization systems.

When a researcher enters the room, an authorized identification system recognizes them and restores their previous working context. The desk becomes an interactive surface displaying an unfinished optical design, while a nearby wall presents a larger three-dimensional visualization of the simulated optical system.

The researcher picks up a physical lens model and places it on the desk. Sensors identify the object and track its position and orientation. A digital model is then associated with the physical object, allowing simulated light paths and optical parameters to be displayed in the surrounding workspace.

When the researcher moves the lens model, the system updates the corresponding digital geometry and recalculates the optical simulation. This creates a direct relationship between physical manipulation and digital computation.

During a discussion with colleagues, relevant design information can be shared across nearby surfaces with permission. Participants can compare simulation results and modify parameters without repeatedly switching between separate applications or devices.

When the researcher leaves the workspace, computational tasks can continue in the background, and the working context can later be restored in another authorized environment.

This scenario would require reliable spatial sensing, distributed computing, interoperable software, real-time simulation, and secure identity management.

Although some of these technologies already exist individually, integrating them into a reliable and unobtrusive environment remains a significant challenge.

More importantly, the goal would not be to fill the room with increasingly sophisticated interfaces. Instead, the environment itself would become a computing system that supports human activities without continuously interrupting them.

This scenario extends Weiser&#39;s concept of embodied virtuality by combining physical interaction with context-aware digital information.

### 7. Conclusion

Mark Weiser&#39;s vision of ubiquitous computing has been partially realized through mobile devices, embedded sensors, wireless networks, and distributed computing infrastructure.

However, the widespread presence of computers should not be confused with the complete realization of ubiquitous computing.

Modern digital systems still require considerable attention, frequently operate within disconnected ecosystems, and introduce important privacy and security concerns.

In my opinion, the future of ubiquitous computing depends on three major transitions: from isolated devices to coordinated environments, from explicit commands to context-aware interaction, and from attention-demanding interfaces to unobtrusive technological assistance.

The imagined intelligent research workspace illustrates how these developments could eventually transform computing from a collection of separate devices into an integrated part of the physical world.

Ultimately, the success of ubiquitous computing should not be measured by how many computers surround us, but by how naturally, reliably, and responsibly they support our everyday activities.

## Lecture 12: The Most Downloaded Applications in a Future Mixed Reality App Store

### 1. Introduction

Mixed Reality (MR) is often discussed in terms of &quot;killer applications&quot; that could motivate consumers to purchase MR head-mounted displays (HMDs). However, I believe that the most popular applications in a future MR ecosystem may not necessarily be those that initially convince people to buy the hardware.

If MR HMDs become as common as smartphones, users will probably expect them to support a wide range of everyday activities, including communication, shopping, entertainment, and personal assistance.

In this situation, the most downloaded applications are likely to be those that combine broad user demand with the unique capabilities of mixed reality.

Unlike conventional smartphone applications, MR applications can integrate virtual content into physical environments, understand spatial relationships, and allow users to interact with digital objects as if they were part of the surrounding world.

I propose three applications that could become particularly popular: MR Spatial AI Companion, MR Virtual Try-On and Shopping, and MR Shared Living and Entertainment.

These applications address three important categories of everyday needs: personal assistance, consumer decision-making, and social entertainment.

### 2. MR Spatial AI Companion

#### 2.1 Application Concept

The first application is an intelligent virtual companion that can understand the user&#39;s physical surroundings and interact naturally within them.

Unlike traditional voice assistants that mainly respond through speakers or smartphone screens, the MR Spatial AI Companion would appear as a three-dimensional virtual character integrated into the real environment.

For example, when a user enters their living room, the assistant could appear beside the sofa or sit on an available chair. Its position would remain consistent with the physical environment as the user moves around.

The assistant could help with everyday activities such as organizing schedules, explaining unfamiliar objects, supporting learning activities, or providing instructions for practical tasks.

A student studying a difficult scientific concept could ask the assistant to display a three-dimensional model directly on the desk. The user could then rotate, resize, or examine the model through gestures.

The assistant could also understand references to nearby objects. For example, if the user points toward a device and asks, &quot;How does this work?&quot;, the system could identify the object and provide relevant explanations.

#### 2.2 MR Interaction and Technical Principles

The application would combine spatial mapping, object recognition, hand tracking, speech recognition, and artificial intelligence.

Spatial mapping would allow the system to construct a representation of the surrounding environment and identify surfaces suitable for placing virtual content.

Object recognition and contextual understanding would help the assistant interpret references to physical objects.

Hand tracking, gaze tracking, and voice interaction could provide several natural ways to communicate with the assistant.

Importantly, the virtual character would not simply float in front of the user&#39;s eyes. It would remain anchored to meaningful positions in the physical environment and respond to spatial changes.

#### 2.3 Expected Popularity

I believe this application could achieve widespread adoption because personal assistance is relevant to almost everyone.

It could support students, professionals, elderly users, and people performing everyday household activities.

Compared with conventional digital assistants, a spatial AI companion could offer more intuitive interactions and reduce the need to switch repeatedly between applications.

Its virtual appearance could also make interactions feel more socially engaging, although this should not replace meaningful human relationships.

The main challenges would involve privacy, reliable environmental understanding, and preventing unnecessary interruptions.

### 3. MR Virtual Try-On and Shopping

#### 3.1 Application Concept

The second application is a mixed reality shopping platform that allows users to experience virtual products within their actual physical environments before purchasing them.

For example, a user shopping for clothing could select a jacket from a virtual catalog. The system would display a three-dimensional representation of the jacket aligned with the user&#39;s body.

As the user turns around or moves their arms, the virtual clothing would update its position and appearance according to the tracked body pose.

The user could change colors, compare styles, and examine the product from different viewpoints.

The application could also support furniture and interior products.

A user considering a new sofa could place a virtual model directly in their living room, adjust its position, and evaluate whether its dimensions and appearance fit the available space.

Instead of relying only on photographs and numerical dimensions, consumers could experience products in the context where they would actually use them.

#### 3.2 MR Interaction and Technical Principles

This application would require body tracking, spatial mapping, three-dimensional rendering, and realistic integration of virtual objects with physical environments.

For clothing, body pose estimation would allow virtual garments to follow the user&#39;s movements. More advanced systems could use approximate body measurements and cloth simulation to produce more realistic visualizations.

For furniture, spatial mapping would identify floors, walls, and available space, allowing virtual objects to be positioned at appropriate scales.

Lighting estimation and occlusion handling would further improve realism. For example, a virtual chair should appear behind a real table when viewed from the appropriate angle.

Users could interact with products through gestures, voice commands, or spatial menus.

However, the system would need to distinguish between visual approximation and accurate physical fit. A convincing virtual representation would not necessarily guarantee that clothing fits perfectly or that a product has the expected material properties.

#### 3.3 Expected Popularity

Shopping is already a major activity on smartphones, and MR could make online purchasing more intuitive and informative.

The ability to preview products at realistic scales could reduce uncertainty, particularly for clothing, furniture, accessories, and home decoration.

Consumers could compare multiple products without visiting physical stores, while retailers could offer more interactive product demonstrations.

Because shopping involves a broad population and repeated purchasing decisions, I believe this type of application could become one of the most downloaded categories in a future MR app store.

Its main limitations would include accurate product modeling, privacy concerns associated with body measurements, and the computational requirements of realistic rendering.

### 4. MR Shared Living and Entertainment

#### 4.1 Application Concept

The third application is a shared mixed reality entertainment platform that allows friends, family members, and virtual characters to interact within persistent spatial environments.

Instead of limiting social interaction to video calls or conventional multiplayer games, users could share immersive experiences that combine physical surroundings with virtual environments.

For example, imagine two friends living in different countries who want to spend an evening together.

They could enter a shared MR environment designed as a virtual campsite beneath the northern lights.

The virtual landscape, campfire, and surrounding objects would be integrated into each user&#39;s physical space. Each participant would see a spatial representation of the other person, allowing them to communicate through voice, gestures, and body movements.

The friends could sit around the virtual campfire, watch a film together, play cooperative games, or interact with a shared virtual pet.

For instance, a virtual fox could move between the participants, respond to their gestures, and react when someone offers it a virtual object.

Unlike a conventional video call, the experience would provide a sense of sharing activities within the same environment rather than simply looking at another person&#39;s image on a screen.

The platform could also support locally shared experiences, allowing several people in the same physical room to see and interact with consistent virtual objects.

#### 4.2 MR Interaction and Technical Principles

The application would rely on spatial mapping, shared spatial coordinates, avatar representation, network synchronization, and interactive virtual objects.

For users in the same location, shared spatial anchors could allow virtual objects to appear in consistent positions from different viewpoints.

For remote users, each participant&#39;s physical environment would need to be mapped into a shared virtual scene. Their movements and interactions would then be synchronized across the network.

Hand tracking and body tracking could support natural gestures, while spatial audio could help conversations feel more immersive.

Virtual characters and objects could respond to user actions and interact with recognized physical surfaces.

The system would also need to manage latency, synchronization errors, and differences between participants&#39; physical environments.

#### 4.3 Expected Popularity

Entertainment and social interaction are already major reasons people use smartphones and digital platforms.

I believe MR could introduce a new form of social entertainment by combining the accessibility of online communication with a stronger sense of spatial presence.

The application could appeal to friends living far apart, families, couples, gaming communities, and users interested in interactive storytelling.

Its popularity would not depend on a single game. Instead, it could provide a platform for many activities, including shared films, cooperative games, virtual pets, interactive stories, and personalized social spaces.

This variety could encourage frequent use and attract a broad user population.

However, realistic social presence would require reliable networking, comfortable HMDs, and careful attention to accessibility, personal boundaries, and user safety.

### 5. Comparison of the Three Applications

The three applications address different aspects of everyday life.

The MR Spatial AI Companion focuses on assistance and productivity. Its main advantage is the ability to provide context-aware support within the user&#39;s physical surroundings.

MR Virtual Try-On and Shopping focuses on consumer activities. Its main advantage is allowing users to evaluate products within realistic spatial contexts before purchasing them.

MR Shared Living and Entertainment focuses on social interaction and recreation. Its main advantage is creating shared experiences that go beyond conventional screens and video communication.

Although their purposes differ, all three applications benefit from the distinctive capabilities of mixed reality, particularly spatial understanding, interactive virtual objects, and integration between physical and digital environments.

I believe that these applications could attract large numbers of users because they address recurring needs rather than depending entirely on technological novelty.

### 6. Conclusion

If MR head-mounted displays become as widespread as smartphones, the most downloaded applications will probably not be limited to specialized professional tools or technically impressive demonstrations.

Instead, popular applications are likely to emerge from everyday activities that people already consider important.

I propose MR Spatial AI Companion, MR Virtual Try-On and Shopping, and MR Shared Living and Entertainment as three promising candidates.

They represent personal assistance, consumer shopping, and social entertainment, while demonstrating how mixed reality could transform familiar activities through spatial interaction.

Ultimately, the success of future MR applications will depend not only on technological sophistication, but also on their ability to provide meaningful, accessible, and enjoyable experiences in everyday life.
